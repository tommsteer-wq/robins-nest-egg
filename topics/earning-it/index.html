Earning It

Where money actually comes from — and the surprising number of ways people get it.

How old is the reader? Tap to see their version.

  • Hatchlings Ages 5–6
  • Fledglings Ages 7–8
  • High Flyers Ages 9–10

🥚 Hatchlings · Ages 5–6

Money doesn't appear by magic. Somebody always did something to get it.

Grown-ups get money by doing jobs. A bus driver drives the bus. A nurse looks after people who are poorly. A baker gets up very early and makes the bread. Someone pays them, and that's their money.

There are hundreds and hundreds of different jobs — probably lots you've never even heard of.

One day you'll be able to earn money too. Some children earn a little by helping out, and some don't. Every family does it differently, and that's completely fine.

Try this: Count how many different jobs you spot in one day — in the street, in a shop, on the telly. How many did you find?

🐣 Fledglings · Ages 7–8

Earning means someone gives you money because you did something useful for them.

That's the whole idea. A job is an agreement: you give your time and what you're good at, and someone pays you for it.

Grown-ups earn in lots of ways. Most have a job working for someone else. Some work for themselves — a plumber, a hairdresser, someone who makes things and sells them. Plenty do several things at once.

Children can earn too, though it looks different. Some get pocket money, some get paid for jobs around the house, and lots of families don't do either. None of those is more right than the others. What matters is the idea underneath: money usually arrives because somebody did something for somebody else.

Some children start tiny businesses — washing cars, making bracelets, selling plants they've grown. The clever bit isn't the thing you make. It's spotting something people actually want.

Try this: Think of one thing people near you find annoying or difficult. Could you help with it? That's the first job of every business there has ever been.

🐦 High Flyers · Ages 9–10

There are really only three ways to earn money — and knowing them changes how you see work.

Most people earn by selling their time. You agree to work certain hours and are paid for them. It's reliable, and it's how almost everyone starts. Its limit is simple arithmetic: there are only so many hours in a week.

Some earn by selling something they make or do — running a business. It's less predictable, and you can lose money as well as make it. But what you earn isn't capped by your own hours, because you can sell to many people at once.

And some earn by owning things that earn. Buy a share of a company, or put money in a savings account, and it can earn while you're asleep — you're no longer trading hours for it at all. That's what Own a Slice and Growing It are about.

Most grown-ups use the first, some use two, a few use all three. None is better than the others — they just behave differently.

One more thing worth knowing: what a job pays isn't a measure of how important it is, or how hard. Pay is mostly about how many people can do that job and how badly someone needs it done. Some of the most valuable work there is doesn't get paid at all.

Try this: Write down three jobs you think matter enormously. Then ask an adult roughly what each one pays. Do your two lists match? Talk about why they might not.
