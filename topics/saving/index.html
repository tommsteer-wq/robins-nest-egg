Saving

Keeping some of your money instead of spending it all — and why that turns out to be one of the most useful things you can learn.

How old is the reader? Tap to jump to their version.

  • Hatchlings Ages 5–6
  • Fledglings Ages 7–8
  • High Flyers Ages 9–10

🥚 Hatchlings · Ages 5–6

Saving is like a little nest for your money!

When you get money — maybe from a birthday, or for helping at home — you don't have to spend it all at once. You can put some of it somewhere safe, like a jar or a piggy bank, and just... let it sit there.

Every time you add a little more, your nest gets a bit fuller. And one day, when it's full enough, you can use it for something really special — something bigger than you could ever buy with just today's money.

Try this: Get a jar. Every time you get a coin, pop it in and watch it grow!

🐣 Fledglings · Ages 7–8

Saving means waiting on purpose — and it's a kind of superpower.

When you save money instead of spending it right away, you're choosing a bigger reward later over a smaller one now. That's genuinely hard to do — even grown-ups find it tricky! — which is exactly why it's worth practising.

A good way to start is picking a savings goal: something you'd love to have, and a rough idea of how much it costs. Then you can track how close you're getting every time you add a bit more.

Money kept in a jar stays exactly the same size forever. Money kept in a bank account can actually grow a little on its own — we'll explain exactly how in the next zone, Growing It.

Try this: Pick one savings goal. Draw a thermometer or a ladder, and colour it in a bit more each time you save.

🐦 High Flyers · Ages 9–10

Saving is delaying spending — and the maths behind it is more interesting than you'd think.

Every time you choose to save instead of spend, you're making a trade: less fun today, for more options later. Psychologists actually have a name for this — it's called delayed gratification, and it's a skill that genuinely gets easier the more you practise it.

Where you keep your savings matters too. A jar under your bed never grows — £20 today is still £20 in five years. But £20 in a bank savings account earns a small amount of interest every year, meaning your money quietly makes more money without you doing anything. It's not huge at first, but it adds up — and in the next zone, Growing It, we'll show you exactly why that becomes a big deal over time.

One more thing worth knowing: banks are required to protect your savings, so keeping money in a proper bank account is genuinely safe, not just convenient.

Try this: Pick a real savings goal with a real price. Work out how many weeks it'd take if you saved £2 a week. Then try £5 a week. How much faster is it?
